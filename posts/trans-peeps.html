Gender Anthropics: An Omissive Lie

August 17, 2026 x min read

Recently, a conversation with my father, an anatomical pathologist, centered around morality of trans people. Many tense dialogues arise from my status as a trans women, however, insight can be found about the traditional conservative—the archetypal Mitt Romney supporter—through these talks. Particularly, he commented about the social perception of others, which provoked some thoughts.

Getting into it, I inquired about why he views hormone replacement therapy as immoral: his response is not the notable part, and I tend to conclude that his moralization often arises from disgust; more salient was an addendum comment after he became frustrated. In essence, he stated that the appearance of a passing trans woman serves as a lie: the imitation of form deceives others omissively. To note, I agree with some generic feminist critique here: his view overlaps with the "panic defense," rooted within objectification of women and misogynistic corollaries. However, abstraction of these definitions lead to worthwhile implications about transphobia, conservative thought, and academia.

You do not have ovaries. Your outer self embodies a lie [from exogenous hormones].

Fix a population which believes traits a, b, c implies facts x, y, z. Consider a person with traits b, c, d who both can and wishes to imitate traits a, b, c. Upon learning of this person, thier imitation becomes viewed as deception to the population: it provides a counterfactual to the implication.

Onto these syllogisms, we must add the amorphous concept of gender to answer what that counterfactual does. I share Judith Butler's view that gender is defined by iterable, performantive acts; I posit it would be beneficial for wider adoption of said views. However, the colloquial, non-academic view of gender differs greatly: most—those sharing traditional, acritical views—percieve gender as a corollary of natal sex. Though some performative notions are preserved in transit to colloquial society—"girls wear dresses, because that it what girls wear"—the average Joe shares little with the academics' definitions.

The prevelance of such information loss has been subject to widespread analysis: I find Jonathan Haidt's "The Righteous Mind" to be the epitome of this. His writing dissects a pathologization of American political groups, to make conclusions that political conclusions of individuals root themselves within patterned normative constructs. Ceterus paribus, I find Haidt's book redundant: a rudimentary understanding of normative ethics, "Manufacturing Consent," mainstream political opinions, and a talk with a dozen politically-active voters could bring about the same conclusions. However, the book garnered a lot of success—enough to be taught in a first-year university course—which illuminates the motivation of intelligentsia to comprehend the average voter. A similar divide exists between gender-critical progressives and the more common, traditional perspectives, however the traditional dissection and pathologization is insufficient. Borrowed from writings on the Anthropic Prinicipal, developing helpful machinery requires acknowledgement that the existence of gender causa sui carries cognitive weight.
